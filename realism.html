Realism

Sub-theories: Structural Realism and Classical Realism

Realism is a school of thought in international relations that emphasizes the competitive and conflictual nature of international politics. Realists believe that states are the primary actors in international relations and that they act in their own self-interest to maximize their power and security. Ideas like power politics and the security dilemma are central to realist theory.

In this school of thought, states act in their own self-interest, focusing power and security in an unpredictable world. States only care about power, seen through the Melian Dialogue in which "The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must."

Sub-theories

Classical Realism

In the offshoot of realism known as classical realism, states only focus on gaining absolute power, which is where states have a really big military through hard and soft power. One example of classical realism is the Melian Dialogue as stated above.

Structural Realism

In structural realism, states seek power in order to survive. This offshoot of realism focuses on relative power as opposed to absolute power. Relative power is a state's ability to influence others by comparing its military, economic, and technological capabilities against another actor. Unlike absolute power, relative power is context-dependent, focusing on gains in comparison to rivals and influencing alliance structures negotiations, and global status.

Does it work?

Realism has been influential in shaping the study of international relations and has provided insights into the behavior of states and the dynamics of international politics. However, it has also faced criticism for being too pessimistic and for failing to account for the role of non-state actors and the potential for cooperation in the international system.

Realism has both strengths and limitations in its approach to understanding international relations. With its emphasis on power and security, it provides a realistic perspective on how states interact in the international system.
